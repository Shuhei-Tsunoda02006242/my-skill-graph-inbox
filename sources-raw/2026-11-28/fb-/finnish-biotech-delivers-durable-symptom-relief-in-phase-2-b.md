A phase 2 trial of Desentum’s birch pollen allergen immunotherapy has hit its primary endpoint, teeing up further study of a short-course medication that could provide a persistent effect.
Existing allergen immunotherapy achieves high response rates and durable effects in people who comply with the regimen. However, the treatment can take several years, leading to poor adherence. The shortcomings create a need for safe, short-course immunotherapies that drive durable responses. Desentum modified the major birch pollen allergen, Bet v 1, to address the unmet need.
Investigators in the phase 2 Basit trial randomized 97 adults with birch pollen allergy to receive the investigational immunotherapy, DM-101PX, or placebo subcutaneously for 10 weeks. Three weeks after the last dose, physicians used an allergen challenge to induce nasal symptoms.
Desentum reported a statistically significant reduction in allergen-induced nasal symptoms compared to placebo, achieving its primary endpoint. The effect persisted through an allergen challenge performed 25 weeks after treatment ended.
“Basit shows that 10 weeks of DM-101PX can produce a clear effect on allergic symptoms that is still present about six months later, supported by a strong and sustained IgG4 response that is generally associated with successful allergen immunotherapy,” Desentum CEO Pekka Mattila said in a statement.
No participants discontinued DM-101PX because of treatment-related effects, and everyone reached the target maximum dose, leading the Finnish biotech to characterize adherence to the regimen as excellent. The biotech plans to share detailed results from the study at upcoming scientific meetings.
Researchers at the Fraunhofer Institute for Toxicology and Experimental Medicine in Germany are testing multiple dosing strategies. Desentum will use the findings to inform the design of a planned dose-finding study.
The Espoo, Finland-based biotech is targeting a sector that Regeneron pulled back from recently. In January, Regeneron started a phase 3 trial of two antibodies—given alone or in combination—in patients with birch tree pollen allergy. However, the pharma terminated the trial months later. The study built on a phase 3 trial that met its primary and key secondary endpoints last year.
