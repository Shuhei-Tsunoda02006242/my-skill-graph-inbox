Fresh off a $150 million IPO, Iambic Therapeutics has unveiled preclinical data for a potential challenger in one of oncology's most competitive drug classes: cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs).
The biotech's newest AI-designed candidate, IAM-C1, curbed tumor growth across multiple hormone-driven cancers and outperformed existing and experimental CDK inhibitors in preclinical studies, according to data presented at the EORTC-NCI-AACR symposium in Spain last week.
During the triple meeting, hosted jointly by the European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC), the National Cancer Institute (NCI), and the American Association for Cancer Research (AACR), Iambic shared promising preclinical data of IAM-C1 in both cell and mouse models.
IAM-C1 is a selective dual CDK2 and CDK4 inhibitor that was designed by what Iambic describes as its “molecular superintelligence program.” CDK2 and CDK4 are cyclin-dependent kinases that help to move dividing cells along the growth cycle and are overactive during cancer cell proliferation.
Targeting the kinases in charge of regulating cell division is a common approach in biopharma. In breast cancer alone, there are multiple FDA-approved CDK4/6 inhibitors such as Pfizer’s Ibrance, Novartis’ Kisqali, and Eli Lilly’s Verzenio.
These kinases have been tricky to target because cancer cells easily find ways to become resistant to CDK4, while using CDK2 as the escape route. The resistance and evasion mechanisms used by cancer cells often drives toxicity in patients, Iambic CEO Tom Miller, Ph.D., explained to Fierce.
The researchers found that IAM-C1 inhibits cancer cell proliferation and depletes cells in S phase—the stage of the cell cycle where the cell duplicates its DNA—in breast, ovarian and endometrial adenocarcinoma cell lines. When evaluating the drug in mouse models of these cancer types, IAM-C1 inhibited tumor cell growth across all cell lines tested, according to Iambic.
The San Diego-based biotech also assessed IAM-C1 head-to-head with other CDK inhibitors, such as Pfizer’s selective CDK4 inhibitor atirmociclib and its FDA-approved Ibrance, and found that IAM-C1 performed better. When they evaluated IAM-C1 in a CDK4 and CDK6 inhibitor-resistant breast cancer line, Iambic found that IAM-C1 demonstrated 78% tumor growth inhibition, compared to 24% with Ibrance.
“I think that represents a level of drug discovery execution on the basis of an AI platform that is really singular in terms of the field and is incredibly exciting,” Miller said. “This is one of the holy grail molecular profiles for the field.”
Iambic has developed an automated, scalable chemistry and biology experimentation platform that is designed to address the totality of drug discovery and development. Once a candidate is designed, it is then moved into biology and chemistry testing conducted by robots in the lab, which then cycle the results and feed back to the AI platform to improve the drug's design.
“It is an automated agentic factory for the creation of new data,” Miller explained. “The factory feeds our AI technology and expands our data moat on a daily basis.”
Another product of Iambic’s platform is IAM-217, which targets kinesin family member 18A (KIF18A), a protein that regulates chromosome alignment during cell division. For cancers driven by chromosome instability, such as breast, ovarian and lung, among others, KIF18A has become a prominent oncology drug target.
At the triple meeting, Iambic presented data showing that IAM-217 given orally to mice had the ability to enter the brain and drive nearly 90% regression in an intracranial breast cancer model of brain metastasis.
IAM-217 has received the green light from the FDA to enter clinical trials, and Iambic plans to start those processes soon.
“We're so excited to bring that into patients with first-to-human dosing in the near term, and so excited to have that IND cleared with the FDA,” Miller said.
Iambic’s lead candidate, a brain-penetrant small molecule inhibitor of HER2 called IAM-1363, is currently in phase 2 trials for HER2+ cancers such as breast and non-small cell lung cancer. The biotech went public in October with a $150 million IPO, with part of the money expected to be used for phase 3 studies of IAM-1363, as well as take an allosteric inhibitor of KIF18A into the clinic.
