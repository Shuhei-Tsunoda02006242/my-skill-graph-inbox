Vyriad's leadership may describe the biotech as a “very quiet company” when compared to some of its more buzzy peers, but the drug developer's partnerships speak for themselves.
The Midwest biotech, which is focused on developing in vivo CAR-T and viral therapies for cancer, bagged its first Big Pharma collaborator in 2019 courtesy of Regeneron. That agreement included launching a phase 2 trial of Regeneron’s blockbuster PD-1 inhibitor Libtayo in combination with Vyriad’s oncolytic virus Voyager-V1 in multiple types of cancer, including colorectal, head and neck carcinoma and melanoma.
Vyriad followed that up with a 2024 pact with Novartis to leverage the biotech's active targeting lentiviral vector platform to discover and develop new in vivo CAR-T cell therapies.
By that point, lentiviruses were already being used to engineer T cells in the lab for approved ex vivo CAR-T therapies. Vyriad’s lentivirus vectors target T cells within a patient’s body and deliver genes coding for chimeric antigen receptors. Once the T cells decipher those genetic instructions, they are programmed to go after targets expressing antigens that match up with the receptors.
Luke Russell, Ph.D., has been with Vyriad since 2018, but recently transitioned to the role of president. Through his decade of experience with the company, Russell has been present and helped negotiate all of Vyriad’s partnerships.
“I was one of very few people inside the company that had the bench lab research experience as well as [the] clinical experience,” Russell told Fierce in an interview at Vyriad's headquarters in Rochester, Minnesota, back in September.
When it came to the Regeneron deal, Russell's mission was to “just make sure that the two companies were in lockstep, manage the decision-making, and be the point person for issues that might come up during the collaboration.”
“I think we formed a really strong working relationship that benefited us and benefited Regeneron in a number of ways,” he added.
For the Novartis agreement, the goal was “to try and bring together these complementary pieces of science,” Russell said.
“We've got this in vivo delivery vehicle; Novartis has outstanding expertise in the CAR-T arena,” he explained. “I think we've been both able to help move science forward much faster than either of us might have been able to do alone.”
At the time of the Novartis deal, the Swiss company hadn't yet followed its Big Pharma peers by dipping into the in vivo space. Since then, Novartis CEO Vas Narasimhan has told Fierce that the company is “continuing to evaluate” the space, although it hasn't yet made the call to take the plunge and buy an in vivo-focused biotech outright.
Still, last month Novartis went deeper into in vivo when the pharma paid $575 million upfront to China-based Abogen Biosciences for an autoimmune disease-focused T-cell engager that uses mRNA to direct the endogenous production of T-cell engagers inside the body.
While Russell was tight-lipped about how Vyriad's collaboration with Novartis is progressing, he told Fierce that the biotech “absolutely love working with our collaborators at Novartis.”
“I think we've got a very good and strong working relationship, and we are making progress towards the clinic,” he added.
“We've been publicly a very quiet company. We're generally not very reported on,” Russell told Fierce. “But I think when we do have an interaction with a Big Pharma company or whoever it might be that's interested in learning more about our science, they are extremely impressed with our team, with the science, with the depth of thought and comprehensive way that we approach problems.”
This approach, combined with “how scientifically strong” the company's workforce is, has “really been the key to our success,” he added.
Vyriad formed in 2016 from a merger of two Mayo Clinic spinouts, Omnis Pharma and Magnis Therapeutics. Since then, the company has raised a total of $85 million in series B funds from the likes of the Mayo Clinic, Regeneron and the Southeast Minnesota Capital Fund.
The biotech's pipeline now includes an in vivo CAR T program and multiple oncolytic virus programs. Its in vivo CAR T program rests on the proprietary lentiviral platform centered on blinded and re-targeted VSV-G delivery.
“Re-targeting viruses and turning them into delivery vehicles really has been a huge focus for the company over the last several years,” Russell said.
VSV-G, or vesicular stomatitis virus glycoprotein G, is an envelope protein found on the surface of the vesicular stomatitis virus that is involved in fusion and viral entry into cells. Because the target receptor for the VSV oncolytic virus is present ubiquitously on cells, it has traditionally been hard to target the virus at a specific cell of interest.
To resolve this problem, Vyriad has introduced a mutation in the VSV-G protein so that it can no longer bind to its receptor and instead re-targets the virus to bind to CD3-positive T-cells. By containing the binding and re-targeting into one construct, “you are maintaining the two natural functions of VSV-G, which is to bind and fuse all in one, rather than trying to separate out those functions,” Russell explained.
Vyriad has recently taken an in vivo BCMA-targeting CAR-T therapy dubbed VV169 into a phase 1 trial for multiple myeloma. What makes VV169 unique is the T-cell specific promoter and the reverse orientation of the CAR construct, which blinds the reader in the viral genome from recognizing the CAR, Russell said. If the viral genome were to read the CAR protein, there is a possibility that it could be expressed and cause adverse events in patients, such as clonal relapses.
G-Link is Vyriad’s CAR-T delivery technology to re-target lentiviral vectors. The platform involves trimeric architecture that enables simultaneous engagement of the three protomers of VSV-G. This allows for high-avidity attachment to the VSV-G protein, selective engagement of T cells and other cell types and normal membrane fusion and preserving of the vector function.
In May, Vyriad made G-Link commercially available to academic researchers and biotechs with the goal of “mak[ing] in vivo gene therapy or gene delivery accessible to the masses,” Russell told Fierce.
Although there has been a recent trend of biotechs turning CAR-T technology toward autoimmune disease, it doesn't sound like Vyriad will be jumping on that bandwagon anytime soon. Instead, the company will retain its focus on oncology, as the “risk-benefit of a cancer patient is much different than an autoimmune patient,” Russell noted.
“Working our way into lower dosing or more tolerable therapies is probably the answer to being able to unlock that patient population also for in vivo lentiviral vectors,” Russell added.
An early readout from the phase 1 VV169 trial in multiple myeloma patients is expected around late 2027. But for the future of Vyriad, Russell is excited to see what other partnerships are on the horizon.
“I would really love to expand the collaborations we have,” he said. “I think the promise of the types of delivery vehicles that we come up with is huge, and our ability to independently develop them at Vyriad is not huge.”
