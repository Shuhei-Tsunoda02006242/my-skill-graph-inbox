When Fierce first met up with Tim Hunt back in November 2025, the CEO of the Alliance for Regenerative Medicine (ARM)—which represents over 400 members, including biotechs—was optimistic that cell and gene therapies were high on the government’s agenda.
Earlier that year, Hunt had been included among the regenerative medicine industry insiders invited to a meeting with recently appointed Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. There were warm words exchanged about the need to accelerate the field, Hunt would later tell Fierce, and in June 2025 he was invited to participate in a gene therapy roundtable hosted by the FDA, where Kennedy asked the assembled scientists and advocates to provide a list of regulations they’d like to see gone.
But by the end of last year, Hunt could already spot some worrying signs about the agency’s approach to rare disease approvals. They included the “first signals” that the FDA would take a harsher view on approving uniQure’s one-time gene therapy candidate for Huntington’s disease, despite the treatment being hailed as a game changer.
“That was one shoe that kind of dropped,” Hunt told Fierce in an interview on the sidelines of London Life Sciences Week last week. “And then we began to hear some concerns from other CEOs in our sector that they were bumping into some challenges.”
Those fears crystallized in January when Atara Biotherapeutics and its partner, Pierre Fabre Pharmaceuticals, accused the FDA of pulling an about-face on Ebvallo when the agency found fresh reasons to reject the Epstein-Barr virus-positive post-transplant lymphoproliferative disease treatment.
Then in February, the FDA rejected Regenxbio’s Hunter syndrome gene therapy. The agency’s rationale hinged on the need to enroll more patients and incorporate an untreated control arm rather than rely on natural history data, requirements that the company had flagged as difficult for an ultra-rare disease population.
The struggles of uniQure, Atara and Regenxbio became “kind of anchors for us,” recalled Hunt, who explained how ARM realized it needed to step up its strategy.
“We did lots of meetings with people inside the FDA, HHS, the White House [and] Congress to try to better understand what was going on and how do we get things from where they were to what we thought would be a much better place overall,” he explained.
“And, yeah, it was not good,” he remembered.
When it came to the White House, the meetings didn’t go all the way up to Kennedy, but they did involve “senior staff,” Hunt said.
“To the administration’s enduring credit, all of the meetings were professional [and] constructive,” he recalled. “They listened. They asked excellent questions. But, clearly, we were frustrated, and I just did not believe it was tenable because we saw some extremely promising medicines that were getting hung up.”
Having previously partnered “very closely” with the FDA on initiatives such as a workshop on the pediatric applications of cell and gene therapy, Hunt said it was “uncomfortable” to be in conflict with the agency’s direction.
“We kind of felt like we weren't left with a choice, and so we had to get much more vocal,” he explained.
To better understand the situation on the ground, Hunt and his team spoke to around 30 CEOs and C-suite execs from ARM's membership to gauge their recent experience with the FDA. Responses generally fell into two camps, he recalled.
“Some would tell us things are going really well. ‘Our [FDA] review team is intact. We’re seeing volatility when we read what’s going on in the news or hear from investors or whatever. But we're doing really well,’” Hunt said.
“Those were welcome conversations,” he added. “But we had more that were, like, ‘We have a problem.’”
So what does Hunt think was the root cause of this U-turn on approving urgently needed drugs for certain rare, degenerative conditions?
“I really think it came down to … a new ideology crept into the FDA,” he told Fierce.
“To do it justice, I think it was people that were very focused on trying to ensure clinical trials were optimally designed and executed to get almost the perfect answer,” he suggested. “But in our world, you know, for too many of these patients, these are last resort, break-the-glass medicines.”
“Very close observers to the cell and gene therapy community, I think, would almost universally acknowledge that this generation of medicines are not perfect,” Hunt admitted. He name-checked safety concerns, efficacy challenges and “legitimate questions about durability of response” that can mean these treatments should not be considered “perfect.”
“As someone in our community said to me, ‘If this generation of medicines was perfect, we'd have a different name for them. We'd call them miracles, right?’”
But a lot of the conditions being discussed in this context are deadly disorders that affect children, Hunt pointed out.
“What we saw [at the FDA] was this very focused approach on methodological rigor,” he said. “And we were arguing, ‘Look, there has to be greater flexibility and a different way to think about it.’”
As a result, Hunt and his team were continually making the case for the value of single-arm trials when it came to rare, deadly diseases where there simply isn't a standard of care to use as the basis for a placebo control.
ARM’s job was made harder by the churn at the top of the FDA, which has been without a permanent commissioner since Marty Makary, M.D., departed in May. Only last week, the White House pulled its nomination for Heidi Overton, M.D., Ph.D., just hours before the Senate was scheduled to vote on her confirmation.
The turnover was equally concerning at other levels of the agency. The FDA's Center for Biologics Evaluation and Research (CBER)—which oversees the regulation of biological products like vaccines and gene therapies—saw its chief Peter Marks, M.D., Ph.D., resign back in March 2025, followed by the turbulent 11-month tenure of Vinay Prasad, M.D. It wasn’t until September 2026 that the agency confirmed that Karim Mikhail would remain as CBER chief on a permanent basis.
In his own interview with Fierce last month, Mikhail pointed to the FDA’s new Rare Disease Framework Initiative as a way to make developing these drugs “more predictable.”
From Hunt’s perspective, Mikhail “immediately steadied the ship” and “got very involved with cell and gene therapy.”
“Karim Mikhail is someone that I think is really blessed with an abundance of common sense and a very practical approach to how these medicines are developed and how do they get approved,” Hunt told Fierce.
Another positive sign for the cell and gene therapy industry, according to Hunt, was Kyle Diamantas’ decision to meet with patient organizations soon after being named acting commissioner of the FDA in May.
“That was a very powerful signal to the community that it's a new day,” he said.
The current class of leaders at the FDA has “corrected course,” according to Hunt, which means ARM is finishing the year in a far more comfortable place then it began. This is reflected in the recent round of regulatory decisions, including uniQure’s Huntington’s gene therapy being given priority review ahead of a May 2027 approval decision, plus an FDA change of heart in agreeing to review Regenxbio’s own filing based on existing studies.
Alongside these decisions is a newfound willingness to hear the views of patients, drug developers and non-profits like ARM, according to Hunt, who described the FDA’s rare disease framework as a “very important initiative.”
“Let’s see how it all plays out, of course, over time,” he said. “But the early reviews [of the current FDA leadership] have been extremely favorable.”
“They’ve been inclusive. They've been communicative,” Hunt added. “That's kind of all you can ask for.”
