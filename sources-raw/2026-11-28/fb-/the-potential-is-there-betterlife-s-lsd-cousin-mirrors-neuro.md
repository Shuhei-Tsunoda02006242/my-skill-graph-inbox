With the boom in researching psychedelics to treat neurological conditions, scientists have begun to explore ways to alter these drugs to remove the hallucinogenic effects while salvaging their neuroplastic tendencies.
These efforts include a Canadian collaboration between Vancouver-based biotech BetterLife Pharma and Carleton University in Ottawa, where a team evaluated the gene changes in mice given BETR-001 versus lysergic acid diethylamide (LSD). BETR-001 is an oral non-hallucinogenic neuroplastogen being developed by BetterLife for migraines and cluster headaches.
The drug is a stereoisomer of 2-bromo-LSD, meaning it has the same chemical structure but has a slight modification to include a bromine on the second carbon. This is a well-known formulation that was studied back in the 1950s.
Early research revealed that this compound, dubbed BOL-148 at the time, did not cause hallucinations like LSD, no matter how high the dose. Researchers then believed it to be “pharmacologically inert” due to this unique property, BetterLife Chief Operating Officer Hooshmand Sheshbaradaran, Ph.D., explained to Fierce.
In the study with Carleton University, which was presented during the Society for Neuroscience annual meeting in Washington, D.C., last week, adult mice were given either LSD or BETR-001 every other day for a total of six days. The researchers found that despite not triggering hallucinogenic effects, BETR-001 still caused similar gene changes to those seen in mice given LSD.
They analyzed changes in cell signaling and expression in brain regions targeted by the compounds and found the cell profiles to be overlapping. Both compounds affected genes associated with synaptic scaffolding and dendritic spine remodeling, processes involved in the brain's ability to adapt and form new connections.
Psychedelics like LSD and ketamine are being widely studied for their ability to cause the brain to adapt and rewire itself, a process known as neuroplasticity.
“We really just started with chemistry,” BetterLife CEO Ahmad Doroudian, Ph.D., told Fierce in an interview. “If you can clean it up and get rid of the side effects, you could have something interesting.”
In 2022, the biotech filed for two patents to manufacture 2-bromo-LSD without using LSD as the starting material. Typically, synthesis of any compounds from LSD can be unpredictable and contain traces of LSD left behind. BetterLife sought to manufacture its drug from a different ergoline, a term for a natural or synthetic chemical compound that resembles neurotransmitters.
The company aims to do more studies to understand the mechanism that BETR-001 is acting on, with plans to file an IND in the first quarter of 2027 to begin trials in healthy volunteers and eventually test the compound in patients with chronic migraine and cluster headaches.
“We are going first with headache, but the potential is there,” Sheshbaradaran told Fierce. “We can always expand to other neuro indications.”
The psychedelics sector, in general, has seen renewed energy this year, with President Donald Trump ordering the FDA to prioritize reviews of psychedelic drugs to treat mental illness. Meanwhile, Eli Lilly acquired AtaiBeckley for $2.8 billion upfront in July, while Compass Pathways came one step closer to a potentially historic approval for its psilocybin formulation for depression after recently completing its rolling FDA submission.
