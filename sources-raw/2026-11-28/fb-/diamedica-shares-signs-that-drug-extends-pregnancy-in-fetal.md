DiaMedica Therapeutics has shared phase 2 results in severe early-onset fetal growth restriction (FGR), reporting that most participants with known delivery dates remained pregnant longer than expected.
The biotech compared patients’ delivery dates to the published median expected time to delivery based on their baseline umbilical artery Doppler abnormalities. They demonstrated that nine of the 12 participants remained pregnant beyond the expected date, while one participant delivered at the expected historical median. Two participants withdrew from the study for social reasons.
DiaMedica provided the historical comparisons for context, cautioning that its study lacked randomized or matched controls. The data come from the first two dose cohorts of an investigator-initiated trial. Patients received DM199, a recombinant form of human tissue kallikrein-1, subcutaneously every three days until delivery.
“These preliminary results provide the first clinical evidence supporting the potential of DM199 to improve uteroplacental perfusion and promote fetal growth while reducing risk of adverse perinatal outcomes in severe early-onset fetal growth restriction, a condition for which there are currently no approved therapies,” DiaMedica CEO Rick Pauls said in a statement.
Prolonged pregnancies provide more time for fetal weight gain. However, while the company reported increased fetal weight, it noted that comparisons were limited by the use of ultrasound-estimated fetal weight during pregnancy and measured birth weight at delivery.
DM199 appeared well tolerated. DiaMedica saw no serious adverse events, episodes of hypotension, treatment interruptions or discontinuations. Safely gaining time in utero can be clinically meaningful, Catherine Cluver, M.D., Ph.D., professor of maternal and fetal medicine at Stellenbosch University and principal investigator of the study, said in a statement.
The FGR study is ongoing. After treating (PDF) six patients in a third dose cohort, the investigator will enroll 12 people to receive the chosen dose in a confirmation cohort. Other parts of the study are testing DM199 in preeclampsia. Stephen Tong, a professor of obstetrics at the University of Melbourne, laid out the overlap between the indications at a DiaMedica event Tuesday.
“They really have a lot of similarities in their disease pathology, in the pathophysiology, in the disease evolution,” Tong said. “They are both placental disorders. We think that the placental pathology arises in early pregnancy. However, while there is overlap, they are different diseases, and you definitely can see one without the other.”
