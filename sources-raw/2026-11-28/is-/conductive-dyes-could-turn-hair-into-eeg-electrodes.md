The most common way to analyze brain signals, electroencephalography (EEG), requires sticking electrodes onto the scalp to detect electrical activity. However, a person’s hair can hinder electrode-skin contact and interfere with the readout. Now researchers suggest a way to solve the problem by using novel dyes.
“In EEG, hair has long been something we have to work around,” says Georgios Spyropoulos, an associate professor of information technology at Ghent University in Belgium. “We asked whether we could make it part of the recording interface instead.”
Brain monitoring often relies on EEG because of its safe, painless, noninvasive nature. However, not only does hair interfere with EEG readings, but the skin’s outermost layer is highly electrically resistant as well. Conductive gels help scalp electrodes pick up brain signals, but these often help make EEG impractical for long-term or real-world use.
“That led us to ask whether hair itself could become useful for recording,” says Xenofon Strakosas, an assistant professor of organic electronics at Linköping University in Sweden.
Conductive Polymer Hair Dyes for EEG
The researchers developed electrically conductive polymer hair dyes, which they call eHAIR. Hair coated with these dyes could provide a stable platform for an electronic interface, without the need for the standard conductive gels, and could create a connection that extends to the root of the hair, in the deeper, more conductive skin layers.
The scientists created two different dyes based on how long they are needed for brain monitoring. One dye was easy to apply onto single hairs or bundles of hairs and easy to wash out, making it useful for quick recordings on demand. The other grew conductive polymers directly on single hair strands, for a semi-permanent coating that could survive hair-washing, making it potentially useful for longer-term monitoring for days or even a week.
“The hardest part was balancing conductivity, flexibility, adhesion, and removability,” Spyropoulos says.
In tests on rats, eHAIR functioned as stable, flexible, highly conductive interfaces for gel-free EEG. The researchers add that their eHAIR is fully compatible with existing EEG hardware, with the dyed hair replacing both the scalp electrodes and conductive gels; the hairs are connected to recording equipment either using metal clips, or by applying the dyes onto hairs and wires to bind them together. The researchers showed that they could even make transistors and amplifiers based on eHAIR, so treated hairs can help process any signals they collect.
“The most direct application is EEG, with potential uses in epilepsy monitoring, sleep assessment, neuroscience research, and brain–computer interfaces,” Strakosas says. He also sees potential applications for other polymer hair coatings similar to the ones used for eHAIR. For example, electrochromic coatings could eventually allow hair to change color in response to a voltage, opening interesting possibilities in cosmetics.”
This work “still has a long, long way to go, but the idea is interesting,” says Jane Huggins, a research associate professor of biomedical engineering and physical medicine and rehabilitation at the University of Michigan, who did not participate in this study. “It would be a great advantage for people using a brain-computer interface in a wheelchair with a headrest or using a brain-computer interface from bed if it were possible to record without adding any perceptible equipment for the EEG recording.”
One concern is that conductive hairs from different spots on a patient’s head could touch and mix their signals. The researchers developed a thin insulating coating to go on top of the conductive dyes which could prevent such crossed wires without degrading eHAIR’s electrical performance.
In the future, the researchers want to test their dyes with different hair types and study how they perform during everyday conditions. “We need to understand how movement, sweat, washing, and hair growth affect comfort and signal quality,” Strakosas says. “The biggest challenges are consistent performance, long-term stability, and safety with repeated use.”
All in all, the hope is the polymer dyes “could make brain monitoring easier to use and fit more naturally into people’s lives,” Spyropoulos says.
The scientists detailed their findings 11 November in the journal Science Advances.
