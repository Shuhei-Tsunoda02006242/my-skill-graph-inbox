Insider Brief
- The Bank of England urged systemically important financial firms to adopt quantum-resistant cryptography ahead of the U.K.’s national timetable, warning that delays at shared suppliers could disrupt services across the sector.
- A sufficiently powerful quantum computer could enable forged digital signatures and expose sensitive financial records that attackers collect today for future decryption.
- The U.K. roadmap calls for migration plans by the end of 2028, priority upgrades by the end of 2031 and full migration by the end of 2035.
The Bank of England urged financial firms with the greatest impact on the stability of the U.K. financial system to move faster than the national timetable for adopting quantum-resistant cryptography, warning that delays at a single technology provider could disrupt services at several firms at once.
In its November Financial Stability Report, the central bank’s Financial Policy Committee made an orderly shift to post-quantum cryptography an immediate priority. The committee had already urged firms to start planning. It says institutions whose disruption would have the biggest consequences for the wider financial system should get ahead of the milestones set out by the U.K.’s National Cyber Security Centre.
The U.K. roadmap calls on organizations to identify where they use cryptography. Then, they are asked to draw up a migration plan by the end of 2028, complete their highest-priority upgrades by the end of 2031 and finish the transition by the end of 2035. The committee called for systemically important firms to move sooner without specifying new dates for them.
What a Quantum Computer Could Put at Risk
Although a quantum computer capable of breaking widely used public-key cryptography has yet to be built, such a machine could make it possible to forge digital signatures or certificates used to verify identities and transactions, the bank said. That would create doubts on the authenticity of financial contracts and the integrity of records that firms need to trust for years.
Some confidential information is already exposed to what the report calls harvest-now, decrypt-later attacks.
In this scenario, an attacker who obtains encrypted records today could hold them until a sufficiently powerful quantum computer can read them. For financial firms, the risk depends in part on how long the information needs to remain confidential. The longer that period, the more important it becomes to protect the data before a machine capable of breaking its encryption arrives.
The Risk of Moving Out of Step
The bank warned that it isn’t just quantum computers that could cause problems. The migration itself could disrupt services, particularly if institutions upgrade in the wrong order or adopt methods that cannot work together. A transaction can go through several firms and systems before it is completed. An upgrade at one link could disrupt the entire transaction if the next link is unprepared. Competition for specialists, suitable software and hardware could slow several firms at the same time.
U.K. regulators’ guidance in the report offers a path on how firms can account for those risks. It calls for an inventory that covers everything from certificates and backups to systems operated by suppliers. It also advises on testing whether upgraded systems work securely with existing services and whether firms can reverse a failed upgrade safely. Some older systems may have to be replaced or retired if they cannot support the new cryptography.
Different national timetables add another complication for firms operating across borders. The report points to Australia’s goal of completing its migration by the end of 2030 and India’s target of upgrading critical information infrastructure by the end of 2028. Those targets apply to different sets of systems. The Bank of England is working with the G7 Cyber Expert Group and other bodies to help keep financial services compatible as countries switch at different speeds.
Within the U.K., the Cross Market Operational Resilience Group has published a method for assessing how well shared services and suppliers are prepared for the transition, according to the report. The bank said it would continue working with industry to identify which dependencies matter most and coordinate when financial firms, payment systems and technology providers make their upgrades
The report also sees longer-term uses for quantum computing in finance, including trading, portfolio optimization, fraud detection and payment processing.
Crypto-agility will need to be central to an organization’s security measures in the future, according to the analysts. The report warned that firms will need to be able to change their cryptography again because standards will likely evolve over time. Systems that are difficult to update could leave firms exposed to new vulnerabilities after the initial migration.
