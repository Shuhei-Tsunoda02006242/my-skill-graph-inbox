When urgent care becomes emergency care
Intuitive Health, a private equity-backed company that runs about 50 hybrid ER-urgent care clinics, was launched on the idea that it could offer an alternative to costly hospital visits, providing under the same roof a clinic for uncomplicated issues and an ER in case of necessity.
But for many patients, the opposite has happened: They came in with simple, urgent care-appropriate conditions but were billed for ER services — think close to $2,000 for an UTI or $7,000 ($2,100 after insurance) for congestion and cough. Company officials say every patient should be asked to sign a form before the “upgrade,” but Tara Bannow found in her investigation this was far from standard practice.
A halt to Medicaid work requirements for medically frail people
A federal judge in Massachusetts has rejected the federal government’s definition of “medical frailty,” ruling that states didn’t have enough time to assess eligibility status. The decision overturns an interim CMS rule that substantially limited the pool of people eligible for exemptions to the work requirements, in particular by requiring that medically frail people demonstrate that their condition prevents them from working. The rule also didn’t list conditions that would automatically be exempt from work requirements.
The lawsuit was brought on by states claiming that CMS didn’t have the authority to narrow the definition of medically frail, and that the rule would create red tape for beneficiaries having to demonstrate their status, as well as an additional burden for state workers. Thursday’s ruling complicates the already tangled web of work requirements, and might lead to a delay in their rollout past the beginning of 2027, when they were slated to go into effect. More here.

Why is colorectal cancer more aggressive in young people? A new database offers clues
People who are diagnosed with colorectal cancer earlier in life, especially younger than 35, often have a grimmer prognosis than people who develop the disease older. Researchers have been trying to understand why. A new “atlas” of 189 colorectal cancer tumors from patients in their 20s to their 90s could help explain the differences in the disease, while controlling for variables that may have confounded previous studies.
“It is devastating to care for young patients diagnosed with often advanced stages of colorectal cancer. These are people in the prime of their life,” Kimmie Ng, the colorectal cancer researcher who authored the database study, told Angus Chen. “That’s what motivates us to do the work, find out what might be causing this, and what unique features we could target so that we can better treat these patients. It’s something that keeps me up at night, and one of the biggest questions facing our scientific community today.”
Ng, who serves as the director of the Dana-Farber Cancer Institute Young-Onset Colorectal Cancer Center, discussed in a Q&A what kinds of insights the database might offer, and what her team is learning about the increasing rate of colorectal cancer in young patients.
Men’s health as gender health
A new series in The Lancet argues for the need to investigate a paradox of men’s health: why men experience worse health outcomes than women, even though they hold undeniable privilege. In their approach, the series authors move away from looking at men’s health as an issue of sex, broadening the frame to focus on gender. “Men’s health is defined and shaped by the culturally defined roles, responsibilities, attributes, and entitlements associated with being (or being seen as) a man, as well as power relations between and among women and men,” they write in the introduction.
A first paper proposes a framework that analyzes men’s health through an intersectional lens, focusing on structural determinants of health. A second looks at the available data about men’s life expectancy and burden of disability, trying — with limitations due to the lack of an intersectional perspective in many of the studies so far — to tease out risk factors and causes. The third paper proposes a political strategy that advances men’s health as a contributing factor to the shared public good.
Discussion of men’s health is wont to raise the question of whether it is detracting from women’s. The authors defend their choice. “A gendered approach to health is not a zero-sum game; it can serve to enhance wellbeing for all,” they write.
