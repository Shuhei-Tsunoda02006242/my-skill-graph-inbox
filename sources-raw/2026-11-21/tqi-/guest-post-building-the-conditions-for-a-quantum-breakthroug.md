Guest Post by Dr. Juan Bernabé-Moreno and Ivan Houlihan
There is a class of problems in materials science that classical computers cannot solve. The math involved grows exponentially more complex as the system being studied gets larger. Add more dimensions to a quantum system or introduce realistic interactions between particles, and the number of variables quickly exceeds what any classical simulation can track with confidence.
Earlier this year, researchers from IBM Research Ireland were part of an international team including scientists from Trinity College Dublin, NIST, and Basque Quantum (BasQ), that published a paper in Nature Communications tackling this challenge.
The paper demonstrated that quantum and classical computers can be combined to study physical systems too complex for either approach alone, with direct implications for nanoscale semiconductor research.
The research centered on a class of interactions, known as Heisenberg-type spin interactions, that describe how the magnetic orientation of one particle influences its neighbors. These interactions arise in a range of physical systems relevant to semiconductor innovation, including quantum dot architectures, single-molecule magnets, and metallic chains.
Quantum dots are tiny semiconductor crystals, often just a few hundred atoms across, whose electronic and optical properties can be precisely tuned by controlling their size. Better computational tools for studying how spin interactions behave in these kinds of systems could accelerate materials research across the semiconductor industry.
The result required 144 qubits on an IBM Quantum Heron processor working alongside classical tensor network simulations. The classical methods can approximate these systems but lose reliability as size and complexity increase. At the largest scales considered, the classical simulations, alone, are not considered reliable.
The breakthrough speaks for itself, but the research environment that made that combination possible is the larger story.
A Different Kind of Research Structure
The researchers work out of a lab that has operated on the campus of Trinity College Dublin for just over a year. In June 2025, IBM Research moved its team from a facility on the outskirts of Dublin into Trinity’s business school, co-located with the university’s research and innovation teams. The move built on a partnership between IBM and Trinity that stretches back more than 25 years.
Relocating to campus has deepened that collaboration. Researchers who previously worked with Trinity faculty across town now share a building with them. Seminars, student supervision, and day-to-day proximity create conditions for the kind of cross-disciplinary work that is harder to sustain at a distance. Since the move, IBM and Trinity have co-authored more than 20 joint publications accumulating over 100 citations.
Recent outputs reflect the breadth of that work. For example, a paper on quantum-centric supercomputing for materials science, co-authored by an international team including researchers from IBM Research Ireland, received a Best Paper award from Future Generation Computer Systems in 2025. A tool for AI risk governance built with Trinity and the ADAPT research center won an AI Ireland award for responsible AI the same year.
Building the Talent Pipeline
Co-location addresses proximity, but attracting and developing world-class talent is another challenge. In 2021, IBM launched a pre-doctoral program, backed by a €1.9M grant from IDA Ireland, that has since hired 20 researchers across Trinity College Dublin, University College Dublin, and Dublin City University.
Unlike a standard PhD fellowship, participants are formally employed by IBM Research Europe, giving them access to IBM’s internal tools and global research network from day one. They contribute to IBM’s research agenda directly, jointly supervised by IBM scientists and university faculty. The program is complemented by annual IBM Research Masterclasses at Trinity, which have brought senior technical leaders from organizations including CERN, Cleveland Clinic, and Imperial College London to Dublin, with several hundred researchers having attended to date, with a new class underway.
IBM’s 70-year history in Ireland established the institutional relationships that made a program like this possible. The company has operated here since 1956 and employs teams across Dublin, Cork, and Galway. The pre-doctoral model addresses something more specific: a structural shortage of industry research scientists in Ireland at the level a world-class lab requires. The program has begun to close that gap, producing researchers whose knowledge stays in Ireland regardless of where their careers take them.
What Ireland’s Research Density Makes Possible
Ireland’s strength in advanced computing reflects decades of sustained multinational investment. The country hosts a high concentration of US technology companies active in AI and advanced computing, creating a deep talent pool and collaborative infrastructure that few EU locations can match.
For IBM Research Ireland, talent is only part of what that investment makes possible. As an English-speaking EU member state with strong institutional ties to the United States, Ireland serves as a practical bridge between American innovation networks and European research funding structures. IBM Research Ireland has put that position to work through participation in two transatlantic research programs coordinated through Trinity. The first is 6G-XCEL, a consortium exploring AI deployment in next-generation mobile communications networks. The second is the European Commission’s Digital Twin Ocean project, which applies AI modeling to marine coastal ecosystems.
IDA Ireland’s role across all of this has been to create the structural conditions for major breakthroughs. Those include co-investment in talent programs, facilitation of university-industry partnerships, and a predictable policy environment that makes a 70-year corporate commitment viable.
What Comes Next
The Nature Communications paper is a milestone in a program that is continuing to grow and flourish. The IBM Research Ireland team is developing new quantum algorithms that combine quantum processors with classical high-performance computing. This includes efforts to solve partial differential equations, a class of problems with broad relevance in semiconductor design, fluid dynamics, and materials modeling. And developing new error mitigation methods to make quantum hardware more reliable before the industry achieves full fault tolerance.
Hybrid quantum-classical computing is beginning to close the gap between research and real-world use, but getting there takes more than hardware. The industry must continue to develop the expertise for the workforce required to develop the needed algorithms, software, and applications.
Behind every quantum breakthrough is a research infrastructure that deserves equal attention. Ireland has been building that infrastructure deliberately, and the results are beginning to show. The model IBM and IDA Ireland have developed in Dublin, embedding researchers inside a university, building a local talent pipeline, and committing for the long term is one the rest of the industry can learn from.
