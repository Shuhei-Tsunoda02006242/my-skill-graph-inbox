With new equipment funding at Penn State and the University of Toronto, Delta Gold Technologies has launched a dedicated quantum sensing program, allowing its gold nanoclusters to get to work on navigation and magnetic sensing while the quantum computing effort continues.
Insider Brief
- Delta Gold Technologies has launched a dedicated quantum sensing program, which it describes as the nearest-term commercial pathway for its gold nanocluster technology.
- At Penn State, a US$500,000 magnetic field instrument, funded through the existing six-year, US$6 million sponsored research program, will test how the clusters respond to controlled 3D field changes. GPS-free navigation is a primary target.
- The University of Toronto has received a C$500,000 budget amendment for atomic-layer fabrication and microscopy equipment, paid from funds Delta Gold has already transferred. The two groups will pursue the sensing work together.
For most of us, day-to-day navigation happens somewhere overhead. Our phones listen to timing signals from satellites up above that trilaterate and pinpoint our location, i.e. GPS.
But as it turns out, we may be better off putting our ears to the ground. The Earth’s magnetic field varies from place to place in ways that can be charted. An instrument sensitive enough to register those variations can locate itself against such a chart without any external signal at all.
Enter quantum sensing.
The same delicacy that makes a qubit hard to protect inside a computer makes it very good at registering small changes outside one. And now, the team at Delta Gold has launched its own dedicated quantum sensing program, along with funding for sensing equipment at Penn State University and the University of Toronto, to explore the possibilities.
Where the Map Began
Delta Gold’s research has, from the outset, run along two parallel tracks joined by a single material.
- At Penn State, Professor Kenneth Knappenberger’s group approaches gold optically, encoding information in the spin of gold nanoclusters and reading it out through polarised light.
- At the University of Toronto, Professor Harry Ruda’s group approaches it electrically, growing atomically thin planar gold structures one layer at a time and addressing their spin states through circuitry.
The case for gold rests on tunability. Rather than depend on a rare defect or an isolated particle, a gold nanocluster’s size, surface chemistry, and attached ligands can be built deliberately, so the property a device needs is designed into every unit rather than sorted for afterwards. The Penn State team has already recorded roughly 40% spin-polarised photon emission from its clusters, alongside gram-scale synthesis under standard laboratory conditions.
For Delta Gold, quantum sensing has moved from the first stop on a roadmap to a funded program in its own right.
Making Sense of Things
A quantum sensor measures its environment by watching how a quantum state responds to it. Magnetic fields, gravity and local chemistry all leave traces that a sufficiently sensitive system can detect. Navigation is the most visible use today, but Delta Gold has also pointed to medical imaging, mineral exploration and battery diagnostics as possible applications.
In each case the engineering task is similar: making sensors stable, compact, and affordable enough to work outside a specialist laboratory.
This is where Delta Gold sees an opening for its nanoclusters. Knappenberger’s view is that their stability as sources of spin-polarised light should make them a robust foundation for sensing, especially for picking up shifts in a magnetic field. The next phase of work at Penn State is designed to put that expectation to the test.
Asked what that could mean in practice, Knappenberger returns to navigation. A device that is stable, straightforward to manufacture, and able to sense small changes in magnetic fields can help aircraft and vehicles position themselves without GPS, all the while using the Earth’s field as the reference.
There is a strategic logic here as well. A gold-based quantum computer is a long-term goal; a sensor is a device that can be specified, tested and licensed considerably sooner. Delta Gold now describes sensing as its nearest-term commercial pathway, and expects the sensing and communications work to support the computing effort rather than compete with it for attention.
Inside the New Program
At Penn State, quantum sensing and communications have been folded into the existing six-year, US$6 million sponsored research program. The central purchase is a magnetic field instrument, budgeted at US$500,000 from the program’s 2026 allocation, which can generate changing three-dimensional magnetic fields around the gold nanoclusters. With it, the group can subject the clusters to controlled field variations and observe how they respond, both for sensing in general and with navigation specifically in view.
The communications work is already underway. For several months, the Penn State team has been running experiments on the quantum output of its clusters, aimed at allowing quantum devices to communicate with one another and building on the same results that led to its three patent applications. Linking quantum devices into networks remains one of the field’s harder open problems, and one under active investigation by groups around the world.
Meanwhile, at the University of Toronto, a C$500,000 budget amendment covers fabrication equipment for growing atomic layers, along with microscopy to measure what those layered structures produce. The equipment is earmarked for quantum sensing devices and will be paid for from funds already transferred: Delta Gold’s total commitment under the Toronto agreement stays at C$3 million, of which C$2 million has been paid. Once the three-year vesting program concludes, the company has the option of adding a further C$500,000 in research funding.
The two groups are not working in isolation. Penn State concentrates on the photonic behavior of gold and Toronto on its electronic behavior. The material is common to both programs and to every patent application filed so far, with the new sensing work pursued collaboratively between them. Ruda sees that collaboration serving two timelines at once. In his account, the joint sensing investigations open up opportunities in the near term while also advancing the two universities’ shared inquiry into whether gold can ultimately support quantum computation.
Delta Gold CEO Mike Jones frames the opportunity in structural terms. Because the company’s technology sits, as he put it, “at the very root of quantum systems”, he argues it has a bearing on communications and networking, on sensing and navigation, and on computing, with each of those markets offering room to establish early intellectual property.
A Golden Future for Quantum Sensing
The immediate question is straightforward: do gold nanoclusters respond to a changing magnetic field cleanly and reliably enough to anchor a working sensor?
The new instrument at Penn State is built to find out, and the Toronto equipment extends the same question to layered gold structures. The details of both approaches remain confidential while patent work continues, but Delta Gold’s new program is determined to pave the way.
