I’m in the somewhat unusual position of basically riding electric bicycles for a living. I’ve been working with e-bikes for nearly two decades, professionally covering the e-bike industry for most of that time, testing hundreds of different models and traveling around the world to ride new e-bikes in some pretty incredible places. Yeah, it’s a tough job – but someone’s got to suffer it.
Of course I don’t just ride e-bikes for work. I ride them recreationally. I ride them for exercise. I ride them for transportation. My family doesn’t even own a car, meaning two wheels handle the vast majority of our transportation.
In other words: I talk the talk and I walk the walk. Or perhaps more accurately, I ride the ride.
And while most of the time all you really need for a good ride is an e-bike and a direction to go in, there are a handful of pieces of gear that I’ve come to rely on over the years. Some keep me safer. Some keep my bikes out of the hands of thieves. Some make rides more comfortable or convenient. And some are just neat little things that I’ve discovered along the way.
This isn’t a list of every bike accessory I’ve ever tested, nor am I saying these are necessarily the absolute best products in every category. These are simply the things that I actually use. Some are a bit pricey, some are dirt cheap (several pieces of gear below are under $5!), but they’re all things I personally rely on and that I recommend to my friends and family. And now, to you.
Seatylock’s FoldyLock Forever and Mason 220
If you own nice e-bikes, you’re eventually going to spend a lot of time thinking about locks. Your lock may even be your most important accessory. Or perhaps I should say “accessories”, because the best way to ensure your e-bike is still there when you get back is to use more than one solid lock.
My go-to options that I grab basically every day for the last couple of years have been Seatylock’s FoldyLock Forever folding lock and Mason 220 U-lock.
For reference, the Foldylock Forever is the world’s strongest folding lock. That’s not just their marketing speak, but rather it’s confirmed by independent testing and certification. It’s the only folding lock in the world to achieve an ART 3-star security rating awarded by the Stichting ART Foundation, an independent Dutch organization that tests and certifies anti-theft locks. It earned that certification three years ago, and no other folding lock has been able to reach that level yet. The only higher ratings ART gives are for locks rated for motorcycles.
Personally, I like folding locks because they pack relatively neatly on the bike while still giving me enough flexibility to work around different racks and poles, or even larger things like trees.
The FoldyLock Forever is the one I reach for when I want serious security matched with more flexibility, while the Mason 220 gives me another beefy option when I know a U-lock will fit what I’m locking to.
The Mason is great as a strong U-lock, but I also like how large it is, letting me lock to wider poles and also a tad bit farther from the pole itself, leaving room for pannier bags and other things that get in the way. Amazingly, it’s also one of the few U-locks I have that can fit over the beefy battery-filled downtube on most of my e-bikes.
I also use the smaller FoldyLock Compact when I want something easier to carry and don’t need quite as much lock.
It’s more affordable and lighter, though it doesn’t have quite the same security level as the bigger and beefier options above.
The Compact does have one other advantage, though: it comes in all sorts of bright colors and even a rainbow option where each link is a different color. That might not be a huge security concern, but it’s fun when you can choose a color to match your bike or mood!
No bike lock makes a bicycle theft-proof. Given enough time and the right tools, a determined thief can defeat virtually anything. The idea is to make your bike enough of a pain in the butt to steal that the thief decides someone else’s bike looks easier. And if they still want your bike (because let’s face it, you’ve probably got an awesome e-bike), then the multiple locks better be good ones so its takes them long enough that they eventually give up.
I also covered Seatylock’s upcoming angle grinder-proof U-locks that I saw at the Milan Motorcycle Show, so I’ll likely switch to those once they come out this Spring. They’re designed to keep motorcycles safe, and come in two sizes: one for motorcycles and a smaller one for bikes.
UNIT 1 helmets
Let’s continue with what may actually be the most important piece of gear for an e-biker: a helmet.
I’ve tested plenty of helmets over the years, but lately I’ve been gravitating toward UNIT 1’s helmets. They’re comfortable, they look good, and I appreciate the extra safety features beyond simply putting a layer of impact protection around my skull.
I particularly like having integrated lighting built right into my helmet, putting lights up high where they’re easier for drivers to see. On models like the FARO, I also appreciate the inclusion of MIPS as well as the handlebar-mounted remote for activating the helmet’s turn signals.
I recently started using UNIT 1’s NEON helmet too, which is lighter and has a really slick magnetic face shield. You barely have to line the shield up; get it close enough and the magnets practically snatch it out of your hand and put it into place. It’s clear enough for night riding but darkens in bright sunlight, and the helmet is rated for faster 28 mph e-bike riding.
There are cheaper helmets out there, of course, but considering how much time I spend on two wheels, a good helmet is one area where I don’t mind spending a bit more.

TriEye rear-view mirror glasses
TriEye glasses are another accessory that sounds a little gimmicky until you actually start using them. Sunglasses with a built-in rearview mirror? I was sure they were going to be a gimmick but they just looked too interesting to pass up, so I offered to test them out.
As it turns out, they’re now the first sunglasses I reach for when I’m riding. They really do work!
They have a tiny rear-view mirror integrated into the glasses, giving me a quick view behind me without having to turn my head all the way around.
It takes a little time to get used to looking into such a small mirror, but once your brain figures it out, it becomes surprisingly natural. I can quickly check what’s approaching from behind while still keeping most of my attention pointed forward.
It’s especially useful when riding on roads where I’m regularly interacting with traffic, though I still give an old-fashioned head check before making any major lateral move.
They’re $99, which isn’t bad for decent sunglasses, though you can get them for 10% off with the discount code EBIKE at checkout.
H2O Audio bone-conduction headphones
I love listening to music and podcasts while riding, but I’m not a fan of plugging my ears while sharing the road with cars.
That’s why I’ve become a big fan of H2O Audio’s bone-conduction headphones. They sit outside my ears and transmit sound through bone conduction, meaning my ears themselves remain open to the world around me. I’ve been testing out the new TRI ULTRA PRO model, and it’s pretty dang amazing! I can hear my music perfectly, but I can still hear all the road sounds, pedestrians, other cyclists, etc.
Of course, that doesn’t mean you can crank the music and magically retain perfect situational awareness. But at reasonable volumes, I can enjoy something in the background while still hearing cars, horns, bells and everything else happening around me.
For longer recreational rides especially, they’ve become one of those things I really appreciate having along. I’ve done rides as long as 3 hours with them and they haven’t felt cumbersome or made my ears sore, so the fit seems great for me.
Knog Oi Prima bell – and my cheap secret AirTag bell
I really like the Knog Oi Prima as a bike bell. It looks good, has a nice low-profile design and does what a bell is supposed to do without looking like you bolted a miniature doorbell onto your handlebars.
Despite its small size, it’s also super loud. And unlike the older ratcheting-style bells (krr-krr-krr-krrring), this is a single thumper, meaning I can ding once quickly for a polite “hello, I’m here!” or several hard and rapid DINGs for a “wtf do you think you’re doing, guy?!”
The bell absolutely disappears on my handlebars, which is good because there’s not much real estate left up there. Really, the only downside I can find is that it’s not cheap, at around $32 depending on current sales.
But I also have another favorite bell that costs a fraction as much.
I found cheap bike bells on AliExpress that have a hidden compartment for an Apple AirTag. From the outside, it just looks like a boring bike bell. Inside, it’s secretly helping me keep tabs on my bike.
And for $4, they’re a pretty awesome deal for a loud bell that hides a tracking device. Of course, you’ll have to fork over another $20 or so for the AirTag tracker, but your e-bike is certainly worth that much.
Which brings me to another part of my bike security strategy.
I hide multiple AirTags on my e-bikes
I don’t necessarily stop at one AirTag. One AirTag is for amateurs. I go with multiple. A four-pack is $79, or around half the cost of a solid e-bike lock.
There are all sorts of clever AirTag hiding accessories available now. In addition to the bell, I’ve used a $5 water bottle-style AirTag holder as well as an $8 rear reflector with a hidden AirTag compartment.
The reflector isn’t the most inconspicuous AirTag hider, but the water bottle holder definitely doesn’t scream “guess what’s actually inside of my molded plastic body!” And for added security, the water bottle holder is held on with Torx bolts. It’s not exactly a security key, but even if a bike thief carries hex wrenches with them, they’re unlikely to have Torx wrenches, meaning that the AirTag will stay on the bike a bit longer while it’s in the hands of a thief.
AirTags aren’t dedicated anti-theft GPS trackers, and Apple intentionally includes anti-stalking features that can eventually alert someone that an unknown AirTag is traveling with them. But I still think they’re a useful additional layer of security, especially because they’re cheap enough to use redundantly.
A bike thief might know enough to search for an AirTag and remove it. But are they going to keep searching after they find the first one? What about the second? Are they expecting three?
That’s the idea, anyway. And since I keep pretty good tabs on my e-bikes, I’ll know pretty quickly if one goes missing. From there, it’s a race between me getting to the thief and him finding all of my AirTags. Good luck, buddy.
Beyond Riders protective riding hoodie
I’m a big believer in wearing proper protective gear when riding faster electric two-wheelers, but I also don’t particularly want to look like I’m headed to a motorcycle race every time I leave the house. That’s one of the reasons I’ve really come to appreciate my Beyond Riders protective riding hoodie.
It looks and feels much like a regular casual hoodie, but it’s designed with motorcycle-style protection in mind, including abrasion-resistant materials and removable impact protectors. Believe it or not, there’s flexible CE Level 2 armor in there on the elbows, shoulders, chest, and spine.
I like that I can throw it on before a ride and get an extra layer of protection without having to wear a bulky motorcycle jacket. It’s particularly nice for faster e-bikes and electric mopeds, where the speeds are high enough that I’d rather have something more substantial than a regular sweatshirt between my skin and the pavement.
