The field of micro- and nanoplastics has drawn a lot of interest in recent years as concerns grow about the impact of contamination in the environment. These tiny plastic particles are created when plastic breaks down, and are usually so small that they are invisible to the naked eye.
Scientists have already identified the presence of these plastics in organs such as the brain. But the chemical makeup of these plastics, how they got there and any links to neurologic diseases are still unknown.
At the annual Society for Neuroscience meeting in Washington, D.C., this month, researchers at the University of New Mexico described how they had identified glossy deposits surrounding blood vessels in the brain. Based on their chemical properties and fluorescent appearance under the microscope, these deposits appeared to be plastics and were highly present in the brains of patients who died of dementia.
Elaine Bearer, M.D., Ph.D., professor of pathology at the university and the study's lead author, explained how her team stumbled upon the discovery while examining brains that were donated by patients with neurodegenerative disorders.
“We haven't considered plastics in our cognitive impairment, in our aging, and how it might accumulate over time,” Bearer said during a press conference at the annual meeting. “We might have to revise a lot of things that we've thought.”
Bearer and her team observed build-ups of non-cellular debris gathered near the blood vessels in the brain. In the past, these artifacts would be disregarded by pathologists because they resemble gunk that gets left behind from the breakdown of red blood cells, Bearer explained.
Upon investigating this further, Bearer discovered that the gunk did not stain positive for a red blood cell marker and might in fact be the presence of plastics in the brain. Using a gas chromatography-mass spectrometry machine—a method that separates the individual chemical compounds and then identifies and measures the amount of each one—the researchers discovered that the chemical signatures matched those of industrially manufactured plastics.
These findings were further validated using fluorescent microscopy, where Bearer found that nearly all the brains with dementia had fluorescent signals, meaning that plastics were present. Interestingly, the molecular structure of plastics should not lead to fluorescence, Bearer explained, but if the plastics were synthesized using heavy metals that were residually present on the plastics, this would give them the ability to fluoresce under the microscope.
Bearer and her team plan to do further studies to identify the plastics, their origin and if there is a causal link to dementia.
“They seem to be related, but whether the disease allows the plastics to get in or the plastics stimulate the disease—we don't know which is the cart and which is the horse,” Bearer said at the conference.
