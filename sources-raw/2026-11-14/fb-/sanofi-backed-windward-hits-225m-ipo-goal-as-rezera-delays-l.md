Windward Bio’s IPO has blown to exactly the spot the Swiss biotech had been aiming for, even as Rezera paused its own listing plan over “market conditions.”
Basel-based Windward announced that it hit its previously disclosed target of offering 10.3 million shares priced at $17 apiece. Windward has the potential to boost its $225 million in gross proceeds by around $26.2 million if underwriters take up their option to buy an additional 1.5 million shares at the same price.
The stock is set to start trading on the Nasdaq on Friday under the ticker symbol “WWB.”
The biotech set out earlier this week that its top spending priority is WIN378, an anti-human thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP) monoclonal antibody that Windward acquired from Kelun-Biotech and Harbour BioMed in a $970 million biobucks deal last year. Since then, the company has taken the candidate into a phase 2 trial in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and a phase 2/3 trial in asthma.
Windward has estimated that it will need up to $227.5 million to complete these studies, as well as kick off a phase 3 trial in asthma, which is due to launch in the first half of next year.
TSLP has become a hot target in immunology, acting as an upstream master switch for the inflammatory cascade. Amgen and AstraZeneca broke into the field in 2021 with the FDA’s approval for the injection Tezspire as an add-on maintenance treatment for severe asthma.
More recently, Upstream Bio’s verekitug has shown Tezspire-like efficacy in asthma, although a 24-week dose—which would be a more direct competitor to Windward’s WIN378—fell short of expectations.
GSK entered the arena in 2024 through its $1 billion upfront deal to acquire Aiolos Bio, which got its long-acting anti-TSLP antibody from China’s Hengrui Pharmaceuticals. Other companies, such as Teva and Blackstone-backed Uniquity Bio, are also in the mix with their own anti-TSLP candidates.
Windward has another bet on TSLP in the form of WIN027, which targets both TSLP and human interleukin-13 (IL-13). The biotech is hoping that this dual targeting has the “potential to deliver deeper and more durable disease control than targeting either pathway alone,” the company explained in its Monday filing.
Sanofi’s TSLP and IL-13 inhibitor aced a pair of mid-stage studies for respiratory diseases earlier this year, but missed on a phase 2 eczema trial.
Windward suggested earlier this week that it may need up to $85 million to run a planned phase 2b study of WIN027 in COPD and launch a phase 2 trial in atopic dermatitis. Windward also acquired that asset from China last year, penning a $700 million deal with Qyuns Therapeutics for the IgG1 bispecific monoclonal antibody.
Then, there’s between $5 million and $10 million that could be used to advance the company’s preclinical bispecific programs. These are led by a next-gen bispecific targeting both type 2 (T2) and non-T2 inflammatory pathways.
The biotech is headed up by CEO Luca Santarelli, M.D., who previously ran VectivBio and Therachon. VectivBio was bought by Ironwood Pharmaceuticals, while Therachon was acquired by Pfizer.
Windward hasn’t had any problems bringing in funds in the past, having secured a $200 million series A in 2025 backed by the likes of Novo Holdings. The company followed that up with a $165 million series B earlier this year—which saw Sanofi’s VC arm hop aboard—and still had $207.3 million in cash, cash equivalents and marketable securities at the end of June.
Alongside the IPO, Sanofi had already agreed to purchase $50 million of Windward’s stock in a private placement.
Rezera reconsiders
While Windward kept its IPO on track, Rezera had second thoughts. The biotech—formerly known as NodThera—outlined last week that it was aiming for a $181 million offering to take its NLRP3 inhibitor into phase 3 trials for a cardiovascular disease.
But a spokesperson confirmed to Fierce on Friday that the Boston biotech has decided to delay the proposed IPO “due to current market conditions.”
“Rezera will continue to evaluate all financing options, including a future public offering, as conditions warrant,” they added.
While biotech IPOs have remained resilient in recent months, there have been a few clouds to trouble market observers, including continuing high oil prices and a recent slump in AI stocks.
In a general note on the biotech sector this morning, investment bank Raymond James pointed out that out of the seven drug developers that IPOed in October, five ended the month with their stock below the offer price. This suggested that “greater access to the public markets has not translated into sustained aftermarket support,” the firm said.
