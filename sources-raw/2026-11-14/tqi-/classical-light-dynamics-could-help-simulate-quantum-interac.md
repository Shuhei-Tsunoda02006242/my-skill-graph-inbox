PRESS RELEASE — An international research team of scientist from Saint Louis University (SLU) and two institutions in Germany has shown that multimode nonlinear optical platforms can be harnessed to induce effective quantum interactions between photons, subsequently thermalizing them.
In a study published in Sciences Advances, a research team from SLU’s School of Science and Engineering (SSE), the Max Born Institute, and Humboldt-Universität zu Berlin has demonstrated that the nonlinear dynamics of classical light can influence single and two-photon quantum states.
The SLU team includes Şahin K. Özdemir, Ph.D., professor and chair of electrical and computer engineering; Ramy El-Ganainy, Ph.D., professor of electrical and computer engineering; and Armando Perez-Leija, research scientist. Other authors include Fouad Chahrour, of the Max Born Institute and Humboldt University of Berlin, and Kurt Busch, professor of theoretical physics at the Institute of Physics in the Humboldt University of Berlin and the Max Born Institute.
Photons stand out as the ultimate information carriers in quantum physics and optical sciences. Possessing exceptional coherence and traveling at the upper physical speed limit, they form the backbone of high-speed data transmission and quantum-enhanced optical technologies. Yet, these advantages are coupled with a fundamental physical limitation – the absence of direct photon-photon interactions.
In “Thermalization of quantum light induced by classical nonlinear wave dynamics,” the research team investigated scenarios where quantum light propagates alongside relatively intense classical light fields within multimode nonlinear optical systems. Through rigorous numerical experiments, the team revealed that single- and two-photon states embedded in these configurations do not merely maintain their initial states or suffer unmitigated decoherence; instead, they thermalize.
This thermalization process is confirmed by the emergence of characteristic statistical distributions, specifically matching Rayleigh-Jeans and Boltzmann distributions, Perez-Leija said. “By leveraging the intense classical wave dynamics to mediate effective interactions, the system successfully bridges the gap between macroscopic nonlinear optics and microscopic quantum mechanics.”
This approach provides a powerful framework for simulating quantum behaviors that were previously considered practically inaccessible in optical setups. Rather than requiring exotic, ultra-strong single-photon nonlinear materials—which are notoriously difficult to fabricate and scale — the method achieves effective quantum thermalization using standard, readily available classical optical structures.
“The implications of this work extend far beyond foundational optics, reaching deep into statistical mechanics and condensed matter physics,” said El-Ganainy. “By establishing that classical nonlinearities can effectively thermalize quantum states, the framework offers researchers a powerful, highly controllable testbed for investigating open quantum systems and many-body thermodynamics.”
Furthermore, this platform paves the way for exploring a richer, broader spectrum of complex quantum phenomena — including aspects of quantum phase transitions — within standard laboratory optical settings.
“By lowering the experimental barriers required to study complex quantum photon interactions, the research opens fresh avenues for light dynamics and deepens our fundamental understanding of how statistical equilibrium emerges in quantum systems,” Ozdemir said.
This research was supported by the US Air Force Office of Scientific Research (AFOSR) and the Army Research Office (ARO).
